Abstract

Traditional robotic manipulation systems rely on rigid articulated linkages that exhibit high positioning repeatability but lack intrinsic compliance, rendering them prone to crushing delicate, fragile, or irregularly contoured objects in automated agriculture, biomedical packaging, and explosive ordnance handling. Soft robotic end-effectors address this limitation through structural deformability, enabling passive geometric conformity and safe contact pressure distribution. However, conventional soft grippers suffer from severe engineering bottlenecks: low payload capacity, material hysteresis, complex tethered fluidic supplies, sensor-skin delamination, and an absence of localized feedback capable of detecting incipient slip without bulky optical apparati.

This report establishes the complete Stage-I research foundation and detailed Stage-II implementation roadmap for a Monolithic 3D-Printed (FDM TPU) Tendon-Driven Compliant Soft Robotic Gripper with Embedded Tactile Sensing. Synthesizing an extensive multi-author literature corpus, the research evaluates fluidic elastomer actuators (PneuNets), shape memory alloys (Nitinol), and cable-driven compliant architectures. To overcome mold-tooling delays and fluid leakage risks, a direct additive manufacturing route using Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU 95A) is formulated, integrating circular flexure living hinges that eliminate mechanical joint backlash while maximizing structural fatigue life.

To provide deterministic grasp autonomy, thin-film Force Sensitive Resistors (FSR402) are integrated flush into the phalange contact pads. An integrated mechatronic architecture centered on a dual-core ESP32 executing FreeRTOS is developed. The tactile signal pipeline decouples static normal contact force estimation (via an active second-order low-pass Butterworth filter at \(f_c = 20\text{ Hz}\)) from dynamic incipient slip micro-vibrations (via a 256-point Fast Fourier Transform analyzing the \(100 - 300\text{ Hz}\) spectral energy band). This enables closed-loop servomotor torque compensation in under 35 milliseconds. A non-linear finite element analysis (FEA) framework utilizing Mooney-Rivlin hyperelastic strain energy formulations is established to predict tip deflection and stress concentrations prior to physical fabrication. Finally, a structured 16-week semester roadmap is detailed, establishing quantitative benchmarks for pull-out retention force, payload-to-weight ratio, and an autonomous slip-prevention demonstration on physical hardware.

Keywords: Soft Robotics, Compliant Mechanisms, Additive Manufacturing, TPU 95A, Tactile Sensing, FSR402, Fast Fourier Transform, Incipient Slip Detection, FreeRTOS, Hyperelastic FEA.

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Background of Soft Robotics and Compliant Manipulation

Robotic manipulation systems have traditionally been engineered around rigid mechanical kinematic chains composed of metallic or hard polymeric links joined by discrete revolute, prismatic, or spherical hinges. Such systems achieve sub-millimeter positioning accuracy, high structural rigidity, and substantial load-bearing capacities when operating in structured industrial environments where target workpieces possess invariant dimensions, known orientations, and high mechanical stiffness.

However, modern automation requirements are rapidly shifting toward unstructured and semi-structured domains, such as the selective harvesting of fragile agricultural produce (e.g., tomatoes, strawberries, peaches), handling delicate specimens in biomedical logistics, sorting irregular post-consumer recycling items, and conducting hazardous explosive ordnance disposal (EOD). In these settings, traditional rigid end-effectors exhibit intrinsic limitations: localized stress concentrations causing workpiece rupture, severe control complexity for sensorless grasping of unknown contours, and dynamic contact chatter leading to dropped objects.

Soft robotics represents a paradigm shift in end-effector design by replacing rigid links and discrete mechanical pins with continuously deformable elastomeric bodies and monolithic compliant mechanisms. By embodying mechanical intelligence directly into the elasticity of the material, soft grippers passively adapt their morphological shape to envelop complex contours upon contact, distributing interaction forces over an expanded surface area.

1.2 Motivation and Industrial Relevance

The primary motivation for this project stems from the critical need for a low-cost, self-contained, lightweight, and sensorized soft robotic gripper capable of executing delicate manipulation while providing real-time feedback regarding contact force and grasp stability.

Specific target applications include:

  • Automated Agricultural Harvesting: Gentle detachment and transfer of soft-skinned fruits and vegetables without inducing bruised tissue that accelerates biological decay.
  • Biomedical and Pharmaceutical Logistics: Sorting and transferring thin-walled glass vials, filled fluid ampoules, surgical specimens, and laboratory glassware where mechanical shock must be strictly eliminated.
  • Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD): Interfacing with uncertain, aged, or structurally compromised explosive devices where uncontrolled impact, spark generation, or excessive clamping force could trigger catastrophic detonation.

1.3 Research Issues and Technical Challenges

While passive compliance provides adaptability, it introduces substantial engineering challenges that do not exist in rigid mechanisms:

  1. Non-linear Continuum Mechanics: Soft elastomeric structures undergo geometric and material non-linearities requiring hyperelastic constitutive formulations.
  2. Hysteresis and Viscoelastic Relaxation: Energy dissipation causes loading and unloading curves to diverge, reducing open-loop repeatability.
  3. Tethering Bottlenecks: Conventional pneumatic soft grippers demand heavy compressors, pumps, and valves, restricting mobile deployment.
  4. Sensory Blindness: Without embedded tactile sensing, the controller cannot ascertain grasp stability or detect slip transitions.
  5. Interfacial Delamination: Embedding planar sensors onto stretching elastomeric skins induces severe peeling shear stresses.

1.4 Broad Objectives

To overcome these interconnected challenges, this research develops a monolithic 3D-printed compliant finger architecture fabricated from Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU 95A), driven by remote base-mounted servomotors via high-strength Dyneema tendons, equipped with embedded FSR402 tactile sensors, and governed by a dual-core ESP32 running FreeRTOS and micro-ROS for autonomous slip detection.

2.3 Research Gaps and Limitations

  1. Research Gap 1: Structural Compliance vs. Payload Capacity: Soft bladders buckle under lateral loads; rigid-compliant hybrid flexures bridge this gap.
  2. Research Gap 2: Sensor Delamination under Cyclic Bending: Planar sensors bonded to stretching elastomeric skins peel under cyclic deformation.
  3. Research Gap 3: Absence of Compact Incipient Slip Detection: Existing slip-detecting grippers rely on heavy optical cameras (GelSight) unsuited for mobile arms.
  4. Research Gap 4: Material Viscoelastic Hysteresis: Open-loop controllers ignore TPU relaxation during sustained grasps.
  5. Research Gap 5: Edge Control Disconnect: Many prototypes lack deterministic onboard real-time operating systems integrated with ROS 2.

4.2 Kinematic Modeling and Living Hinge Synthesis

The finger is modeled as a monolithic beam with three localized circular flexure notches replicating human MCP, PIP, and DIP joints. According to Howell's Pseudo-Rigid-Body Model (PRBM), the equivalent torsional spring stiffness \(K_\theta\) is expressed as:

\[ K_\theta = \frac{E \cdot w \cdot t^{2.5}}{9 \pi \sqrt{r}} \]

where \(E\) is the instantaneous flexural modulus of TPU 95A, \(w\) is finger width, \(t\) is flexure throat thickness (1.6 mm), and \(r\) is notch radius (2.5 mm).

Figure 4.2: Kinematic profile of the monolithic compliant finger under cable tension.

4.2.1 Geometric Link Lengths and Kinematic Architecture

The compliant finger architecture adopts an anthropomorphic 3-phalanx kinematic configuration. The monolithic flexible body is partitioned into three discrete functional phalanx segments interconnected by thin circular living hinge flexures:

  • Proximal Phalanx (\(L_1 = 35.0 ext{ mm}\)): Anchored directly to the rigid palm base flange; initiates primary angular deflection during early tendon stroke.
  • Intermediate Phalanx (\(L_2 = 35.0 ext{ mm}\)): Medial link segment that conforms around the lateral flanks of the grasped workpiece.
  • Distal Phalanx (\(L_3 = 35.0 ext{ mm}\)): Fingertip segment incorporating an integrated \(5.0 ext{ mm}\) rounded tip and a precision circular recessed pocket (\(\phi = 18.3 ext{ mm}\), depth \(0.6 ext{ mm}\)) configured to seat the Interlink FSR402 thin-film tactile sensor flush with the grasping surface.

Accounting for the two notch opening gaps (\(g_1 = g_2 = 5.0 ext{ mm}\)) and the distal fingertip extension, the total effective unbent beam length is \(L_{ ext{total}} = 115.0 ext{ mm}\). The finger cross-section features a width of \(w = 20.0 ext{ mm}\) and a height of \(h = 16.0 ext{ mm}\), providing high out-of-plane torsional rigidity against lateral twisting during asymmetric grasping while permitting compliant in-plane deflection. When assembled in a 3-finger radially symmetric array on an \(80.0 ext{ mm}\) pitch circle diameter (PCD) palm base, the fingers achieve a maximum tip curl angle of \(pprox 82^\circ\) under a \(12 - 15 ext{ mm}\) tendon pull, providing an envelope grasp diameter of \(\phi = 25 - 85 ext{ mm}\).

4.2.2 Mechanical Design Constraints and Payload Specifications

To guarantee long-term fatigue durability, reliable load holding, and repeatable actuation, the compliant gripper design is governed by strict mechanical, manufacturing, and operational constraints:

  • Structural Stress & Fatigue Constraints: During peak bending, tensile and compressive stresses concentrate at the throat of the circular flexure hinges. The maximum allowable Von Mises stress is constrained to \(\sigma_{ ext{max}} \le 22.0 ext{ MPa}\). Compared to the ultimate tensile yield strength of 3D-printed TPU 95A (\(\sigma_y pprox 48 - 52 ext{ MPa}\)), this enforces a structural factor of safety \(SF \ge 2.2\), ensuring that hinge deformation remains purely within the reversible elastic regime without yielding, micro-tearing, or low-cycle fatigue over \(>100,000\) flexing cycles. The maximum outer-fiber bending strain is limited to \( arepsilon_{ ext{max}} \le 45\%\), far below the material's failure elongation (\(>450\%\)).
  • Additive Manufacturing Constraints: To eliminate anisotropic delamination, all fingers are designed to be printed flat in the horizontal X-Y build plane. This ensures that the primary tensile bending stresses along the living hinge throat align parallel with continuous, uninterrupted extruded polymer road contours rather than across inter-layer Z-boundaries. Infill at the hinge throats is specified as \(100\%\) concentric.
  • Tendon Actuation & Transmission Limits: Actuation is executed via single-acting high-strength Dyneema braided cable (breaking strength \(>250 ext{ N}\)). Return extension is fully passive, powered by the stored elastic strain energy of the deformed living hinges (\(E_{ ext{strain}} = \sum rac{1}{2} K_{ heta, i} heta_i^2\)). To mitigate friction losses along the internal routing conduits, the channel is lined with micro-PTFE tubing (\(\mu_f \le 0.08\)), ensuring that nominal tendon pull remains \(T_{ ext{nom}} pprox 12.5 ext{ N}\) with a peak operating ceiling of \(T_{ ext{max}} \le 25.0 ext{ N}\).
  • Payload Capacity & Dynamic Grasp Specifications: The gripper is engineered to handle a rated dynamic payload of \(800 - 1500 ext{ g}\) (\(0.8 - 1.5 ext{ kg}\)) across cylinders, spheres, and rectangular objects, with a peak static envelope holding capacity of up to \(2.0 ext{ kg}\) (\(2000 ext{ g}\)). With a total estimated gripper self-mass under \(450 ext{ g}\) (including 3 TPU fingers, PLA palm, servo mounting brackets, and spools), the design achieves an exceptional payload-to-weight ratio exceeding \(3:1\) (and up to \(4.4:1\) in static lock), outperforming conventional fluidic elastomer grippers (\(<1.5:1\)). Controlled normal clamping force spans \(0.1 ext{ N}\) (for delicate workpieces such as raw eggs and ripe fruit) to \(20.0 ext{ N}\) (for robust industrial parts), coupled with a dynamic slip-reflex latency under \(35 ext{ ms}\).

4.3 Grasping Mechanics, Contact Sequence, and Target Workpieces

4.3.1 Underactuated Mechanical Intelligence and Multi-Phase Contact Sequence

Unlike conventional rigid industrial grippers that demand multi-axis servomotor coordination and complex inverse kinematic trajectory planning for every unique workpiece contour, the proposed soft robotic finger utilizes underactuated mechanical intelligence. A single tensile tendon actuates three independent revolute degrees of freedom (MCP, PIP, and DIP living hinges). The grasping progression evolves across five discrete mechanical phases:

  1. Phase 0 (Equilibrium Rest Configuration): The Dyneema tendon is slack (\(T = 0 ext{ N}\)). All three living hinges remain unbent in their straight manufactured posture (\( heta_1 = heta_2 = heta_3 = 0^\circ\)), establishing an open grasp envelope (\(\phi pprox 90 - 110 ext{ mm}\)).
  2. Phase 1 (Free In-Flight Flexure): The base servomotor rotates the drive spool, generating cable tension \(T\). This applies an inward bending moment \(M = T \cdot r_{ ext{cable}}\) across all three flexure notches simultaneously via the \(6.0 ext{ mm}\) neutral-axis offset. All phalanges flex inward proportionally to their torsional compliance (\(K_{ heta, i}\)).
  3. Phase 2 (Proximal Contact and Kinematic Motion Arrest): As the finger sweeps inward, the proximal phalanx (\(L_1\)) or intermediate phalanx (\(L_2\)) impinges upon the exterior surface of the workpiece. The normal contact reaction force creates an opposing moment \(M_{ ext{contact}} = F_n \cdot d_{ ext{contact}}\) that balances the tendon moment at the proximal hinge (\(K_{ heta, 1} heta_1 + M_{ ext{contact}} = T \cdot r_{ ext{cable}}\)), arresting further angular deflection of the proximal link.
  4. Phase 3 (Continuous Routing and Distal Adaptive Curling): Because the braided Dyneema tendon slides freely through low-friction micro-PTFE sleeve liners (\(\mu_f \le 0.08\)), the continuing spool rotation channels the remaining tensile force downstream past the arrested proximal phalanx into the unrestrained intermediate and distal living hinges (\(K_{ heta, 2}\), \(K_{ heta, 3}\)). The intermediate and distal phalanges curl automatically around the irregular contour of the object until multi-point contact equilibrium is established. This passive morphological conformity occurs purely through elastic structural compliance, requiring zero sensory feedback or algorithmic path planning.
  5. Phase 4 (Stable Grasp Lock and Tactile Sensor Activation): The distal phalangeal contact pad presses flush against the workpiece surface, transmitting contact normal force directly into the embedded Interlink FSR402 thin-film sensor. As the calibrated normal force achieves the target setpoint (\(0.5 - 20.0 ext{ N}\)), the FreeRTOS motor control task transitions the servomotor into static holding torque mode.
  6. Phase 5 (Passive Strain-Energy Elastic Release): When release is commanded, the servomotor unwinds the tendon spool. The elastic strain energy stored in the deformed TPU living hinges (\(E_{ ext{strain}} = \sum_{i=1}^3 rac{1}{2} K_{ heta, i} heta_i^2\)) acts as an internal distributed restoring spring, instantly popping the fingers back to their straight resting posture without requiring reverse pushrods or dual-acting actuation.

4.3.2 Grasping Modalities: Power Envelope Grasp versus Precision Pinch Grasp

The kinematic architecture of the 3-finger gripper supports two primary grasping modalities depending on target workpiece dimensions and fragility:

  • Power Envelope Grasp (Form Closure): Suited for medium-to-large objects (\(\phi = 25 - 85 ext{ mm}\), mass up to \(1.5 - 2.0 ext{ kg}\)). The three phalanges completely wrap around the workpiece boundary, maximizing contact surface area. High geometric form-closure and distributed frictional engagement lock the object securely against external dynamic accelerations during manipulator transport.
  • Precision Fingertip Pinch Grasp (Force Closure): Suited for small, thin, or delicate workpieces (\(\phi = 5 - 25 ext{ mm}\), e.g., thin electronic components, test tube necks, pens, and coins). Only the distal phalangeal contact pads (\(L_3\)) press against opposing sides of the workpiece. The high static friction coefficient of 3D-printed TPU (\(\mu_s pprox 0.6 - 0.8\)) provides non-slip stability under low normal clamping forces (\(0.1 - 2.0 ext{ N}\)), preventing structural damage to brittle workpieces.

4.3.3 Bio-Inspired Anthropomorphic Equivalence and the Human Tactile Reflex Arc

The design of the compliant gripper embodies direct bio-inspired structural and functional parallels to the human hand:

  • Kinematic Skeletal Equivalence: Replicates the three-phalanx kinematic chain of the human index finger (Proximal, Middle, and Distal Phalanges) and three rotational joints (Metacarpophalangeal [MCP], Proximal Interphalangeal [PIP], and Distal Interphalangeal [DIP]), replacing mechanical pins and bearings with monolithic circular flexure living hinges.
  • Flexor Tendon Sheath Biomechanics: Emulates the human deep flexor tendon (flexor digitorum profundus) running through its synovial flexor sheath (vagina fibrosa digitorum) by routing high-tensile Dyneema cable through low-friction micro-PTFE conduits embedded within the finger core.
  • Cutaneous Mechanoreceptor Reflex Arc (Meissner's Corpuscles): In human glabrous skin, Fast-Adapting Type I (FA-I / Meissner) mechanoreceptors do not measure static clamping pressure; instead, they fire rapid bursts of action potentials in response to high-frequency shear micro-vibrations (\(100 - 300 ext{ Hz}\)) generated during the microscopic slip of papillary epidermal ridges across the object surface. This sensory volley triggers an involuntary spinal reflex arc that boosts grip force within \(30 - 40 ext{ ms}\) before macro-slip occurs. The proposed mechatronic system mirrors this biological reflex: the FSR402 sensor detects high-frequency tactile micro-vibrations, the ESP32 performs on-chip 256-point Radix-4 FFT spectral analysis in the \(100 - 300 ext{ Hz}\) band on Core 0, and triggers a sub-35 ms servomotor torque compensation interrupt on Core 1.

4.4 Non-linear Hyperelastic Constitutive Formulation

To accurately capture large strain behavior in planned BTP-II finite element simulations, TPU 95A is represented via the 2-Parameter Mooney-Rivlin strain energy density function \(W\):

\[ W = C_{10}(I_1 - 3) + C_{01}(I_2 - 3) + \frac{1}{D_1}(J - 1)^2 \]

where \(C_{10} = 2.45\text{ MPa}\), \(C_{01} = 1.15\text{ MPa}\), and \(D_1\) represents material incompressibility. Hexahedral SOLID186 elements with quadratic displacement interpolation are utilized with large deflection formulation enabled.

4.5 Tactile Sensing, Filtering, and Slip Detection

The FSR402 sensor voltage divider signal is decoupled into static and dynamic regimes:

  1. Static Normal Force: Processed via a 2nd-order active Sallen-Key low-pass filter (\(f_c = 20\text{ Hz}\)) to eliminate motor noise while preserving gripping force.
  2. Dynamic Incipient Slip: Sampled via 12-bit ADC at \(1\text{ kHz}\). An on-chip 256-point Radix-4 FFT computes spectral power in the \(100 - 300\text{ Hz}\) band on ESP32 Core 0. When spectral power exceeds a dynamic threshold, a high-priority interrupt commands the Core 1 control task to increase servomotor PWM torque within < 35 ms.

In strict compliance with academic research standards and BTP-I evaluation guidelines, it is explicitly confirmed that Stage-I represents the literature-review-completed and conceptual architecture decision stage. In Stage-I, no SolidWorks 3D CAD models were constructed, no Ansys FEA simulations were executed, and no physical prototypes were fabricated or tested. Stage-I work strictly encompasses the comprehensive literature review, multi-criteria methodology selection, analytical kinematic formulations, hyperelastic constitutive modeling selection, embedded sensing architecture design, and the semester-wise implementation roadmap. All 3D CAD modeling, non-linear FEA solver execution, physical 3D printing, sensor calibration, and slip prevention trials are planned and scheduled for Stage-II (BTP-II).

5.2 Planned Kinematic and FEA Simulation Protocol for BTP-II

Based on preliminary pseudo-rigid-body analytical formulations, a flexure throat geometry with thickness \(t \approx 1.5 - 1.8\text{ mm}\) and notch radius \(r \approx 2.5\text{ mm}\) is targeted to achieve conformal tip curl for envelope grasping of objects between 25 mm and 85 mm in diameter. In the planned BTP-II simulation campaign (Weeks 4–6), non-linear hyperelastic FEA will be executed in Ansys Mechanical using the 2-Parameter Mooney-Rivlin constitutive formulation. The simulation protocol will apply displacement-controlled tendon retraction up to 15 mm, performing mesh convergence with higher-order hexahedral elements down to 0.4 mm local notch sizing to verify that peak Von Mises stresses remain well below the ~50 MPa tensile yield limit of TPU 95A, guaranteeing high fatigue life prior to physical 3D printing.

Stage-I successfully established the research foundation and planned architecture for the soft robotic gripper:

6.3 Expected Outcomes and Final Demonstration

The culmination of BTP-II will be a live demonstration presented to Prof. Sankha Deb and the evaluation committee featuring:
