Part C – Individual Technical Preparation & Professor Q&A Defense

1. Shinde Sanchali Sanjay – Opening Anchor, Tactile Transduction & Feedback

Core Concepts & Defense Focus: 1. Soft robotics continuum compliance vs rigid point contact • 2. Piezoresistive Quantum Tunneling in FSR402 (\(R \propto F^{-\gamma}\)) • 3. Frequency-domain signal decoupling (Quasi-static 0–20 Hz vs Dynamic 100–300 Hz) • 4. Sallen-Key 2nd-order Butterworth LPF (\(f_c = 20\text{ Hz}\)) • 5. 256-point Radix-4 Real FFT (\(E_{\text{slip}} = \sum_{k=25}^{76} |X[k]|^2\)) • 6. Dual-core FreeRTOS task segregation (Core 0: 1 kHz DMA/FFT vs Core 1: 100 Hz Servo/micro-ROS).
Q1: Piezoresistive FSR sensors are known for severe resistance drift and hysteresis. How can you rely on them for precise robotic grasping?
Answer: "We do not rely on raw FSR resistance for absolute, high-precision force metrology. Instead, our architecture decouples the signal: for quasi-static clamping, we calibrate a voltage divider with a non-inverting op-amp to maintain a safe threshold window. More importantly, for slip prevention, we measure dynamic AC micro-vibrations in the frequency domain (100–300 Hz). Viscoelastic drift occurs over seconds (<0.1 Hz), which is completely rejected by our dynamic baseline AC-tracking filter."
Q2: Why is incipient slip characterized by the 100–300 Hz frequency band?
Answer: "When an elastomeric finger contact pad begins to slip across a grasped object, microscopic surface asperities undergo rapid stick-slip transitions before gross macroscopic displacement occurs. Extensive experimental literature in tactile biomechanics (e.g., Howe & Cutkosky; Dahiya et al.) proves that these micro-vibrations resonate within the 100 to 300 Hz acoustic emission band. Macro manipulation movements occur below 20 Hz, providing a clean spectral separation."
Q3: Why run a 256-point FFT on the microcontroller instead of simple time-domain derivative thresholding (dF/dt)?
Answer: "Time-domain differentiation (\(dF/dt\)) is extremely vulnerable to high-frequency electrical noise, robot arm vibrations, and motor PWM spikes, producing rampant false-positive grip tightening. By transforming the signal into the frequency domain via a 256-point FFT, we compute spectral energy strictly within the 100–300 Hz window: \(E_{\text{slip}} = \sum_{k=25}^{76} |X[k]|^2\). This acts as a robust narrow-band energy detector, achieving high sensitivity to slip while rejecting out-of-band mechanical shocks."
Q4: Why partition the ESP32 firmware into FreeRTOS tasks across separate cores?
Answer: "The ESP32 possesses two Xtensa 32-bit LX6 cores. Computing a 256-point FFT and sampling the ADC via DMA at 1 kHz is computationally intensive. If executed on a single core alongside servomotor PWM updates and micro-ROS serial publishing, the processor would suffer thread blocking and timer jitter. We lock high-speed acquisition and FFT processing to Core 0, while Core 1 handles 100 Hz servomotor PID control and micro-ROS telemetry independently, guaranteeing hard real-time determinism."
Q5: How will you demonstrate autonomous slip prevention in your final BTP-II defense?
Answer: "The gripper will hold a smooth cylindrical object vertically with minimal baseline clamping force. A digital linear actuator will apply an increasing downward pull force on the cylinder. As micro-vibrations begin, the ESP32 FFT algorithm will detect the energy spike in the 100–300 Hz band and trigger a closed-loop servomotor angle step within 35 ms, automatically clamping tighter and preventing the object from dropping. The live force and spectral energy stream will be displayed simultaneously on a ROS 2 dashboard."

2. Saurav Shekhar – Actuation Schemes & Compliant Mechanisms

5 Core Concepts to Master: 1. Kinematic mobility: rigid vs compliant continua • 2. Howell Pseudo-Rigid-Body Model (\(K_\theta = \frac{E w t^{2.5}}{9\pi \sqrt{r}}\)) • 3. Capstan friction accumulation (\(T_{\text{distal}} = T_{\text{base}} e^{-\mu_f \sum \theta_i}\)) • 4. Moment arm around notch throat vs motor torque • 5. Passive elastic return vs antagonistic cabling.
Q1: Why choose tendon-driven actuation over fluidic PneuNets if fluidic grippers offer superior passive contour wrapping?
Answer: "While PneuNets provide continuous fluidic compliance, they require bulky external compressors, valves, and fluid tethers, preventing mobile arm deployment. Their thin membranes are also vulnerable to puncture and have payloads under 400 g. Tendon-driven TPU grippers mount all motor mass at the base, achieve payload-to-weight ratios exceeding 3:1, run on DC battery power, and deliver deterministic holding forces."
Q2: In Howell's PRBM, what governs your selection of notch radius r = 2.5 mm and throat thickness t = 1.6 mm?
Answer: "Throat thickness \(t\) governs bending stiffness exponentially through the \(t^{2.5}\) term in Howell's formulation. If \(t < 1.2\text{ mm}\), the finger exhibits lateral buckling under load. If \(t > 2.0\text{ mm}\), required actuation torque exceeds servo stall limits (\(20\text{ kg}\cdot\text{cm}\)). A thickness of \(1.6\text{ mm}\) with notch radius \(r = 2.5\text{ mm}\) minimizes stress concentrations while ensuring reliable elastic restoration within the fatigue limit of TPU 95A."
Q3: How do you prevent cumulative friction losses from choking cable tension at the distal fingertip?
Answer: "Under the Capstan equation, tension decays exponentially with cumulative bend angle: \(T_{\text{distal}} = T_{\text{base}} e^{-\sum \mu_f \theta_i}\). If Dyneema cables rubbed directly against raw 3D-printed TPU ridges, \(\mu_f\) would exceed 0.35, losing over 60% of tension. We insert smooth micro-PTFE tubing (OD 2 mm, ID 1 mm) along conduits, reducing \(\mu_f\) to ~0.08 so over 85% of motor torque reaches the distal contact pad."
Q4: Why use Dyneema cable instead of stainless steel wire rope or nylon monofilament?
Answer: "Dyneema is an Ultra-High-Molecular-Weight Polyethylene fiber. Compared to steel wire rope, it has zero plastic bending fatigue over small radii, eliminating kink fractures. Compared to nylon, Dyneema has near-zero elastic elongation under load (<1.5%), eliminating control backlash and tension creep during sustained holding."
Q5: How will you measure actual pull-out resistance force during BTP-II testing?
Answer: "We will clamp standardized 3D-printed cylinders (diameters 30, 50, and 70 mm) within the gripper at calibrated tendon tensions. A digital force gauge mounted to a linear lead-screw test rig will apply an axial dislodging pull at 5 mm/s until slip occurs. The peak load cell reading prior to displacement gives the static retention pull-out force."

3. G. Chandana – Materials Science & Additive Manufacturing

5 Core Concepts to Master: 1. Thermoplastic Polyurethanes vs Thermoset Silicones • 2. 2-Parameter Mooney-Rivlin model (\(W = C_{10}(I_1-3) + C_{01}(I_2-3) + \frac{1}{D_1}(J-1)^2\)) • 3. SOLID186 20-node hexahedral elements avoiding shear locking • 4. Anisotropic FDM printing & horizontal layer alignment along tensile stresses • 5. Fatigue life estimation & Safety Factor (>2.2).
Q1: Why did you select TPU 95A over softer grades like TPU 85A or silicone Ecoflex 00-30?
Answer: "While TPU 85A and silicones are softer, their flexural modulus is too low to prevent out-of-plane torsional buckling under heavy loads. Silicone casting also requires time-consuming mold machining and degassing. TPU 95A provides an optimal balance: it has an elastic modulus of ~26 MPa, offering enough compliance for living hinge flexures while providing the structural stiffness needed to support payloads up to 1.5 kg without twisting."
Q2: Why use a 2-Parameter Mooney-Rivlin model instead of Ogden or Yeoh models in Ansys?
Answer: "The 2-Parameter Mooney-Rivlin model accurately characterizes hyperelastic behavior up to ~100% tensile strain, which fully envelops the maximum strain observed at our living hinges (35–45%). Higher-order models like Ogden require extensive biaxial, planar, and volumetric test data to prevent mathematical instability. Mooney-Rivlin constants (\(C_{10} = 2.45\text{ MPa}, C_{01} = 1.15\text{ MPa}\)) are well-characterized and numerically robust in Ansys Mechanical without risk of run-away divergence."
Q3: How do you address the notorious issue of TPU filament jamming in 3D printer extruders?
Answer: "TPU filament has low column stiffness and buckles easily between drive gears and the hotend melt zone in Bowden setups. We mandate a direct-drive extruder where the gap between the dual-drive hobbed gears and the melt zone is constrained to <5 mm. We also restrict print speed to 20 mm/s, disable retraction over short paths, and pre-dry filament at 65°C for 6 hours to eliminate moisture-induced void foaming."
Q4: How do you guarantee that living hinges will not delaminate along layer lines during repetitive cycling?
Answer: "FDM components fail predominantly in inter-layer delamination under tension. We orient the fingers flat on the print bed along the X-Y plane. This ensures that cyclic tensile and compressive bending stresses act parallel to continuous extruded filament strands, entirely avoiding tensile loading across the inter-layer Z-axis. Printing at 225°C with 100% infill ensures thermal remelting and full polymer chain entanglement."
Q5: Has Ansys FEA already been run, or is this an analytical framework?
Answer: "In strict adherence to academic standards and BTP-I guidelines, we have only decided what we will do. We have not created any SolidWorks CAD models or executed Ansys FEA simulations in Stage-I. We have selected the Mooney-Rivlin constitutive formulation and established the simulation strategy; full CAD modeling, mesh generation, and non-linear solver execution are scheduled for Weeks 1 through 6 of BTP-II."
